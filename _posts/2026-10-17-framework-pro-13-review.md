I placed my Framework 13 Pro order on 22 July 2026, the same day Framework announced [LPCAMM2 price hikes on their Intel boards](https://frame.work/blog/updates-on-memory-pricing-and-navigating-the-volatile-memory-market). That's not a coincidence. The price hike pushed the build I wanted out of my range, so I switched to an AMD configuration and sourced my own RAM. By the time the laptop arrived in September, I'd saved enough to also grab a 2TB NVMe and still spend less than my original build would have cost. *Before the computer even arrived, its composability had saved me significant money.*

![Framework 13 Pro](images/framework.jpeg)

## Why Framework?

The build runs the AMD Ryzen AI 9 HX 370 rather than the Intel Core Ultra Series 3 that Framework's marketing leans on, for reasons covered above. Dollar-for-dollar there are better deals out there: Dell XPS machines with the same chips for slightly less money (without the thermal throttling), or Lenovo's spec- and price-comparable 15" ThinkPads. But the new ThinkPad design has a hideous notch, Copilot buttons in place of Alt, and no trackpoint. Each alternative comes with its own tradeoffs.

What sealed it was upgradability. Owners of existing Framework 13s can [upgrade their machine to be a Pro](https://frame.work/laptop13pro?tab=upgrade-to-pro). If component pricing returns to earth, I can switch to an Intel chip with LPCAMM2 RAM without buying a whole new computer. This was a purchase made with a 5- or even 10-year ownership horizon in mind.

## Which OS?

I plan on writing more about this another time, but I settled on CachyOS. I love Arch: I've been using it for close to a decade, including on a few servers where frankly it's a pain in the ass. But midway through setting up LUKS I realized I'd rather spend my time on other things. CachyOS just works. It solves so many usability headaches from a scratch Arch install that I can't justify going back. 

## The Good

The build quality of the Pro is the closest to MacBook Pro quality I've encountered. It's lightweight and solid, with no flex anywhere that matters. The screen hinge opens one-handed without the base lifting and goes a full 180° flat. The trackpad is the best non-Apple trackpad I've used; the haptics are fantastic and work great under KDE. The keyboard has 1.5mm of key travel and holds up well for long typing sessions.

The onboard Radeon 890m GPU holds its own for gaming. I plan on doing some quantitative analysis in the future, but I get ~33 FPS on the Cyberpunk 2077 built-in benchmark with FSR4 on medium settings. Brand new titles like Castlevania: Belmont's Curse are running at ~80-90 FPS. The native Bloodborne port gets over 60 FPS.

One of the Framework's coolest features is the swappable ports. I work at an ISP and regularly need an RJ45 connector for all sorts of tasks. Being able to hot-swap out a (eg) HDMI connector for 2.5G ethernet port when going on a tech visit is invaluable. The modularity and form factor are just so convenient in a working environment.

## The Expected

Battery life on my AMD chip is a bit worse than the advertised ranges. In an office setting, my KDE battery estimates are ~9-10 hours with a full suite of productivity software open. I can probably get these numbers up a bit, but definitely not able to reach the ~17 hours that people are reporting on their Intel chips.


## The Weird

The area where the palms rest collects hand-sweat like nobody's business. Because it's a shiny black, moisture just reflects *brightly.* It's something I've grown self conscious about when people enter my workspace. I've never used a laptop with this nasty quirk before.

The 3:2 screen aspect ratio has taken me longer to get used to than I expected. It's great for single-window focus, but after years (decades?) of 16:9, I've grown accustomed to a 50:50 split between two windows on my primary display. I've been re-experimenting with sway and cosmic after a couple years on KDE; I miss i3 tiling, but I think that will help with the space management.

A month in, the bet looks smart. The hardware is genuinely great, Linux support has been almost entirely painless, and the modularity paid for itself before I even turned it on. The palm-sweat thing is real and gross and I think about it every time someone walks into my office. 

Overall: 5/5.
